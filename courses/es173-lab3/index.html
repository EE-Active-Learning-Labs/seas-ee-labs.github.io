    3

    Light meets a band gap

    A photon is absorbed only if it carries at least the band gap energy. Try it.

    A photon’s energy depends only on its wavelength. If that energy is at least the band gap Eg, the photon can lift an electron from the valence band into the conduction band and leave a hole behind. That electron and hole pair is what a photodiode turns into current. If the energy is smaller than the gap, the photon has nothing to excite and passes through.

    E (eV) ≈ 1240 / λ (nm)    so    cutoff λc ≈ 1240 / Eg

    Jump to a lab flashlight

    Flashlight markers are approximate peak wavelengths. Each flashlight emits a band of wavelengths, so use the measured spectra in Appendix 3 of your handout for real numbers, especially the long wavelength edge of each one.

    Two things to notice

    • Every photon above the gap makes at most one electron and hole pair. The extra energy, E − Eg, is lost as heat within picoseconds.
    • So for the same optical power, longer wavelengths deliver more photons and can give more current, right up until the photon energy falls below the gap and the response collapses.
    4

    Where the light is absorbed

    Above the gap, the question becomes how deep the light gets before it is absorbed.

    Light that can be absorbed fades exponentially as it travels into the material, I(x) = I0 e−αx. The absorption coefficient α depends strongly on wavelength. The penetration depth 1/α is where about 63 percent of the light has been absorbed. In silicon, blue and UV light is absorbed in the first fraction of a micron, while red and infrared reach many microns deep.

    Depth matters because only pairs made in or near the junction’s depletion region are collected as current. Pairs made right at the surface often recombine before they get there. Pairs made far below the junction may recombine too.

    5

    How light shows up in the IV curve

    A lit photodiode is a dark diode plus a photocurrent. Watch the curve shift.

    In the dark, a PN diode follows the diode equation. Light adds a photocurrent Iph that flows the opposite way, in the reverse direction, so the whole curve shifts down.

    I = I0 (eV/nVT − 1) − Iph

    The shift is easiest to see in reverse bias, where the dark current is tiny. That is why you may need to zoom the current range in WaveForms, as you did in Lab 1. The band gap also sets the dark curve. A smaller gap means a larger dark current I0 and a lower turn-on voltage, so a small-gap diode can show the same photocurrent on top of a bigger background.

    How the measurement works

    The Studio sweeps the voltage across a 100 Ω resistor in series with your device. It measures the voltage across each, works out the current from Ohm’s law, I = VR/R, and plots that current against the device voltage.

    Single runs one sweep. Run repeats until you press Stop. Export with File, then Export, as CSV data or an image.

    Build the circuit on the Studio breadboard to match the example in the lab and the diagram in Appendix 1 of your handout. Ask a TF if anything looks different.

    If something looks wrong

    Common snags and what to try. When in doubt, ask your TF.

    The curve does not change with the light

    Zoom the current range way down and look in reverse bias. Bring the flashlight closer and aim it at the active area between the gold fingers. If nothing changes for any source, check your probe contact with a fresh sweep.

    The curve is a straight line through zero

    Both probes may be touching metal that is shorted together, or you are measuring only the resistor. Lift and replace the top probe on a gold contact.

    The curve is flat at zero everywhere

    You likely have an open circuit. Check that each probe touches metal, the jumpers are seated, and both scope switches are on.

    The trace is noisy or jumps around

    Room lights and flickering screens add signal. Shade the sample with your hand or a card, keep the probe cables still, and average a few sweeps.

    Our results flip sign compared with the plots here

    Swapping the probes flips which side of the diode is positive. That is fine. State your polarity in the report and keep it the same for every measurement.

    We cannot tell whether the IR flashlight is on

    Look at it through a phone camera, which often shows IR as a purple glow. Do not look at it directly.
